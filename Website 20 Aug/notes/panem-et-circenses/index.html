Panem et circenses

The crowd applauds one side or the other, holding a banner because that's easier than finding out what's true.

Something in the way outrage moves through social media deserves more scrutiny than it usually gets. The same category of injustice can be treated as unforgivable or shrugged off entirely, and what decides the reaction seems to depend more on who is involved than on what actually happened.

Every few weeks a new cause takes over the feed, and people who understood almost nothing about the underlying situation a week earlier are suddenly certain, and vocal, about who is right and who is guilty. That speed is worth noticing. A stable, personally held sense of right and wrong does not usually form that fast. What forms that fast is agreement with whatever narrative has already been approved by everyone around you.

Part of what makes this uncomfortable is the selectivity of it. Injustice did not begin the week it started trending. Most of what we buy and wear was made somewhere, by someone, under conditions we would rather not look at closely. Conflict has been a near constant somewhere in the world for as long as any of us have been alive, and it rarely gets this kind of attention unless the parties involved fit a story we already like telling. None of the institutions we casually call the good guys have a spotless record, and pretending otherwise does not make the discussion more honest, only less so.

Social media rewards a particular kind of moral performance: you need to look right more than you need to be right. Whether a position is actually defensible matters less than whether it is currently fashionable to hold. That is not a new human failing, but the speed and scale of the feed make it much harder to notice while it is happening to you.

What should not get lost in any of this is who actually pays the price when things go wrong. It is never institutions or ideologies. It is families, small businesses, ordinary people trying to hold a life together in conditions they did not choose. Whatever position anyone takes on the argument of the week, that cost deserves more attention than the argument itself usually gets.

In the middle of all this, most people end up cheering for one side of a contest they do not fully understand, holding a banner because holding a banner is easier than doing the work of finding out what is actually true. That is not new either. It is just easier to do at scale than it used to be, and considerably harder to notice you are doing it.
